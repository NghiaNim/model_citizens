How the model works

  1. Polls. A weighted average for each race: recent polls count more (14-day decay), larger samples count more, partisan sponsors count half and are adjusted by their measured bias (D-sponsored +2.1, R-sponsored −2.2), and repeat pollsters are discounted. Multi-round states use the final-round head-to-head.
  2. Fundamentals. State lean from the last two presidential results, incumbency, and the fundraising ratio, fitted within each cycle. A one-unit log ratio of money is worth 5.4 points, but this reflects candidate strength as much as money buying votes.
  3. National environment. Estimated from the race polls themselves: +3.9. As a cross-check, a D+8 generic ballot implies +4.1 on the same scale.
  4. Combining and error. The two are weighted by precision, with error terms fitted by maximum likelihood. Close to Election Day, polls take 96 to 98% of the weight wherever they exist. Fundamentals only matter in the unpolled safe states.
  5. Simulation. 400,000 correlated elections with fat-tailed errors and shared national, regional and partisan-lean shocks.
  6. Calibration. A final adjustment to probabilities, fitted on Senate and governor races together. It is nearly neutral (k = 1.05).
  7. Combining sources. An equal-weight average on the log-odds scale of my model, the ratings and the market. They all read the same polls, so the average doesn't count as stronger evidence than its parts.
margin_i ~ w_i · polls_i + (1 − w_i) · fundamentals_i + shocks_i polls_i = weighted_avg + 0.167 · undecided_i · lean_i (undecideds break toward the state's lean) Var(poll) = 3.15² + 3.62²/n_eff + (0.15 · polls_i)² (error grows with margin) shocks = national(sd 2.43, t8) + lean(sd 0.60) + region(sd 0.13) + race(t12) P(win) = logistic(k · logit Φ(margin / sd)) (k = 1.05 combined; Senate alone 1.50, governors alone 0.80; 1 if independent)

For independents I shift by −4.85 points (past comparable races, shrunk 25%) with a 4.5-point shared uncertainty. I dropped two earlier features after the governor test: a term letting leads grow beyond their poll margin, and a separate calibration for trailing incumbents. Trailing-incumbent races now only block updates; they no longer change the probabilities.

  • Sharpening failed to replicate. The frozen Senate calibration scored significantly worse on governors than a milder one (log-loss difference +7.96, 90% CI +3.03 to +13.18). The governor-only fit actually calls for less confidence than the raw simulation.
  • Lead growth failed to replicate. Senate margins ran 1.10 to 1.15 times the polled margin; governors ran 0.97 to 1.05.
  • The trailing-incumbent effect didn't stand out. Trailing incumbents' challengers won 7 of 9 in governor races, about the same as other leaders (76%). Pooled with Senate that's 18 of 26 (69%), so the win rate is still modest, but I can't claim it's different from other close races.

Governor races are a different kind of contest: they're less tied to national party and their errors are larger (5.4 points against 4.3), so some of this gap may be real. I averaged the two instead of throwing out either.

What should make you doubt this

  • A shared polling miss is the main risk. If polls overstate Democrats by 5.3 points, as they did on average in 2020, Talarico falls to about 28%, Brown to 20%, and El-Sayed to about 44%.
  • The calibration is a compromise. Senate alone says k = 1.50 and governors alone say 0.80, and the 1.05 I use is a pooled figure. If Senate races really are more predictable, these numbers are too cautious; the model ranges show how much.
  • Some choices came after seeing data. I made about 15 historical comparisons and tried three versions of the trailing-incumbent adjustment. I also wrote the decision rule after preliminary runs, so it isn't a true pre-registration. The governor test is the one properly out-of-sample check, and it's what cut the raises.
  • Small samples. The independent adjustment rests on four races. The Democrats-leading-in-red-states group has seven Senate races, all won, but the most recent is from 2012, so it says little about today's polling.
  • Data limits. 2026 polls come from Wikipedia tables, not a pollster archive. I found no final generic-ballot average, so D+8 comes from mid-to-late October trackers. The national House margins behind the +4.1 cross-check are from memory, not a downloaded source. The FEC file's coverage date didn't parse, so 2026 fundraising may be slightly out of date. Governor incumbency was inferred from past winners, which misses successors. None of these drives a polled Senate race much, because polls take over 96% of the weight there.
  • Not modeled. Early-vote data (up 50% in Texas and 44% in Maine per AP, but turnout doesn't say who voted for whom), pollster quality scores, and news from the last few hours.

Decision rule

I changed a non-100 number only if all four conditions held: the three-source average differs from yours by at least 4 points; the model and ratings are on the same side of your number; the race isn't a trailing-incumbent or independent race, where history gives weak support; and the pick stays the same. A polled 100 stays if the average is at least 99.5%, otherwise it's rounded to that average. Unpolled safe races keep their earlier values, because governor polling says nothing about races without polls.
