The 2026 United States Senate election in Nebraska will be held on November 3, 2026, to elect a member of the United States Senate to represent the state of Nebraska. Republican incumbent Pete Ricketts is seeking his first full term. He is being challenged by independent former union leader Dan Osborn.

In the May 12 primaries, Ricketts won the Republican nomination with 81.8% of the vote. Former pharmacy technician Cindy Burbank won the Democratic nomination with 89.2% of the vote over pastor William Forbes, before dropping out of the general election in July to support Osborn.

Republicans have not lost a Senate election in Nebraska since 2006. The last time an independent won a Senate election in the state was in 1936.

Background

[edit]

Republicans have not lost a Senate election in Nebraska since Democratic incumbent Ben Nelson was re-elected to a second term in 2006, defeating future senator Pete Ricketts in his first political campaign.[1] The last independent to win a Senate election in Nebraska was George W. Norris when he ran for a fifth term in 1936 after previously serving as a Republican.[2] This election will be the first in which a state did not have a Democratic candidate in back-to-back US Senate election cycles.[3][4] Nebraska is one of three states (along with Idaho and South Dakota) where the Democratic nominee has withdrawn from the race.[5]

Independent

[edit]

In the 2024 United States Senate election in Nebraska, union president Dan Osborn ran as an independent against Republican incumbent Deb Fischer, losing by fewer than seven points in one of Nebraska's closest Senate races. Osborn significantly outperformed Democratic nominee Preston Love Jr., who lost the concurrent special Senate election to Pete Ricketts by more than 24 points.[17]

In early 2025, Osborn formed an exploratory committee for a potential 2026 run.[18] In June 2026, he submitted a petition to appear on the general election ballot as an independent,[19] which was verified the following month.[20]

Although Osborn is not affiliated with the Nebraska Democratic Party, the party is supporting his candidacy, citing their greater alignment with his platform.[21]

Democratic primary

[edit]

Background

[edit]

Jane Kleeb, the chair of the Nebraska Democratic Party, said the party did not intend to recruit a candidate, similar to the Nebraska Senate race in 2024.[23][24] In August 2025, the party endorsed Dan Osborn, an independent candidate.[25]

In March 2026, the party called for Democratic candidate William Forbes to withdraw, with Kleeb accusing him of trying to "trick voters" and opposing abortion access. She urged Democrats to support Osborn instead.[26][27] In an interview with CNN, Forbes acknowledged that he had voted for Donald Trump in multiple elections, but insisted that he was a lifelong Democrat.[28]

After Forbes entered the race retired pharmacy technician Cindy Burbank launched a primary candidacy endorsed by the state party. On March 17, 2026, Secretary of State of Nebraska Bob Evnen removed Burbank from the ballot following a complaint from Nebraska Republican Party that alleged she was not a good-faith candidate.[29] Burbank filed a lawsuit in Lancaster County District Court immediately following the removal.[30] On March 19, 2026, a District Court judge dismissed Burbank's lawsuit.[31] On March 23, 2026, the Nebraska Supreme Court ruled that Evnen missed a deadline to consider complaints and reinstated Burbank to the ballot.[32]

Burbank stated in April that she would consider leaving the race to "unify the votes" against Ricketts.[33] She said in a phone interview a week before the primary that she intended to withdraw from the general election and would support Osborn if there was "no clear path to victory".[34] She filed to be removed from the ballot on July 17,[35] which was confirmed by Secretary of State Evnen on July 21.[36]

After Burbank's withdrawal, Forbes filed a lawsuit which claimed the Nebraska Democratic Party must have a Senate candidate.[37] An initial ruling by a Lancaster County judge stated that the attempt to force the party to field a candidate was unlikely to succeed and raised "serious constitutional concerns."[38] The case was dismissed on August 26, 2026.[39]

Third parties

[edit]

America First Party

[edit]

America First Party organizers submitted completed petitions to form a new party on July 15, 2026; registered voter signatures required to do so were verified by the Nebraska Secretary of State on August 7, 2026.[42] After a ruling by the Nebraska Supreme Court on August 26, 2026, against the Nebraska Republican Party objections (which claimed the party's name would cause confusion for voters) the secretary confirmed the party's formation.[43] Chuck Conboy, an account manager, is the party's nominee in this race.[44]

Legal Marijuana NOW primary

[edit]

Prior to the primary, Mark Elworth Jr., the former chair of the Legal Marijuana NOW party, accused Mike Marvin of being a "Dan Osborn plant".[45] In addition, Republicans accused Marvin of planning to drop out and endorse Osborn if he won the nomination. Marvin has denied the allegations.[46] Cindy Burbank, the Democratic nominee, paid for Marvin's $1,740 filing fee when the secretary of state refused to take his own check, which had the incorrect amount.[47][48] Marvin did not submit the paperwork to be removed from the ballot before the August 3 deadline,[49] but withdrew and endorsed Osborn on August 21, citing a health condition. He will remain on the ballot.[50]

Nebraska Working People Party

[edit]

Nebraska Working People Party organizers submitted completed petitions to form a new party on July 14 and was verified by the Secretary of State on August 7, 2026.[42] After a ruling by the Nebraska Supreme Court on August 26, 2026, that the Nebraska Democratic Party's objection (claiming that the name is too similar to the Working Families Party) had been filed too late, the secretary confirmed the party's formation.[43] Robin Richards, the party's chair, is the party's nominee in this race.[51]

General election

[edit]

According to The New York Times, the cost of living, inflation, AI data centers, and income inequality have been leading issues during the election campaign.[52]

Osborn vowed to ban "corporate PAC donations" and "a congressional stock trading ban". He "also raised concerns about farm bankruptcies and large investors buying agricultural land." He is a critic of AI data centers, and has cited it as an instance of the powerful plotting to undermine the contributions of everyday individuals.[53] Ricketts supports the construction of AI data centers and called it a matter of "national security".[54] On the campaign, he said that he favors the development of artificial general intelligence.[55] Osborn portrayed him as an "out-of-touch billionaire".[53]

If elected, Osborn vowed to form an "Independent Senatorial Caucus", separate from both major parties.[56]
