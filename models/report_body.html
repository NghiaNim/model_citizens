How the forecast is built

flowchart LR

  C[Results and money] --> D[Fundamentals]
  B --> E[Model]
  D --> E
  E --> F[Calibration]
  G[Forecaster ratings] --> H[Calibrated ratings]
  I[Kalshi and Polymarket] --> J[Market price]
  F --> K[Log-odds average]
  H --> K
  J --> K
  K --> L[Prediction]
  1. Poll average. Recent polls and larger samples count more. Sponsored polls count half and are corrected for their measured lean (D +2.1, R −2.2). Ranked-choice states use the final-round match-up.
  2. Fundamentals. State lean, incumbency and fundraising, fitted on 452 past races. The national environment comes from the race polls: +3.9, against +4.1 implied by a D+8 generic ballot.
  3. Model. Polls and fundamentals are weighted by precision. Polls get 96–98% wherever they're plentiful, so fundamentals drive only unpolled races.
  4. Independent candidates. Shifted by −4.85 points with wide uncertainty, because comparable candidates ran behind their polls.
  5. Calibration. A final adjustment fitted on Senate and governor races together (k = 1.05).
  6. Forecaster ratings. Fourteen forecasters' ratings, mapped to probabilities using 267 Senate races from 2010–24.
  7. Markets. The average of Kalshi's midpoint and Polymarket's price.
  8. Final number. An equal-weight average of model, ratings and market on the log-odds scale.

The 50–90% range is well calibrated (71.7% predicted, 73.1% actual, n=104). The 90–99% range looks underconfident on Senate data (95.8% predicted, 100% actual, n=80), but governor races are why it isn't sharpened.

Governor races: the out-of-sample test

Calibration settings fitted on Senate races were run on 333 governor races that played no part in fitting them. Two features failed and were dropped: leads growing beyond the polls, and sharper Senate-only calibration (log-loss difference +7.96, 90% CI +3.03 to +13.18). Senate alone gives k = 1.50 and governors 0.80, so the forecast uses the pooled 1.05. Governor races are less tied to national party and have larger errors (5.4 points against 4.3), so some of this gap may be real.

{{GOV}}

Market prices

Markets can reflect information polls miss, such as late news and early voting. I tested them on 32 Senate races in 2024, using Polymarket prices from the morning of the election. Polymarket alone was the weakest input, and adding it barely changed the score, so this shows that including it does no harm, not that it helps. Its favorites at 90% or higher won 24 of 24. This is one cycle, and it uses the final calibration, which was partly fitted on 2024.

{{MARKET}}

Forecaster ratings

The ratings beat the model on the same 273 Senate races (Brier 0.040 against 0.046), which is why they get equal say. Equal weights are used because one cycle of market data can't support fitted weights, and simple averages are hard to beat in forecast combination (Clemen, 1989).

Hand-set settings

These were set by hand and then tested against results in competitive races. Error barely changes across the range, so no setting drives the forecast. A 21-day window scores best, but 60 days keeps polls available for thinly polled races.

What could be wrong

  • A shared polling miss is the main risk. If polls overstate Democrats by 5.3 points, as they did in 2020, the model alone drops Talarico to about 28%, El-Sayed to 44% and Brown to 20%.
  • Calibration is a compromise. If Senate races really are more predictable than governor races, these numbers are too cautious.
  • Some numbers rest on thin evidence. That applies most to IA (pick decided by ratings), LA (three polls), NE (four comparable races), SC and ME.
  • The sources aren't independent. Model, ratings and market all read the same polls, so their average isn't stronger evidence than its parts.
  • Choices followed the data. Settings were hand-set first and tested afterward, and I made about 15 historical comparisons while developing this. Only the governor races and the 2024 market prices were not used in building the core model.
  • Some inputs are approximate. 2026 polls come from Wikipedia tables. D+8 comes from October trackers, with no final generic-ballot average found. House margins in the environment cross-check were entered from memory. The FEC coverage date didn't parse. Governor incumbency was inferred from past winners.
  • Market gaps. Louisiana has no Kalshi price in the feed. Alaska's Kalshi market (71–72) wasn't merged because it's listed by party; this changes the result by under 0.1 point.
  • Not modeled. Early-vote data, pollster quality scores, and news after the data was collected.
